THE PERMISSION LIE

one of the deepest, most subtle prisons i live in is the belief that there is nothing i can do about 
situations. i cannot act, choose, or become without some permission. i was taught this early, without 
words sometimes, simply through tone, expectation and example, that my desires, instincts, or 
sense of self must be filtered through approval of some others - a parent, a teacher, the community,
later on even the imagined judgement of our bank account balances.
When I finished secondary school few years ago, so many things were changing fast. I already knew things about the 
world because i have actually learnt those things (from school ond online). My waec result had 9 credits, and
i had been exposed to blockchain technology and other basic computing. yet there seemed to be not much 
i could do about the situation. it became obvious that so many things i wanted wont be done simply 
because i had no money, and  all i wanted to do was get into IT and the world of computing, not state service or 
any thing like that. But in the streets around me, computing has already been mystified into criminology by 
the majority of people who get into it to make it quick. Now my family would not set me up with a laptop because 
they did not believe i will do anything useful with it. it is understandable because where i am from, the 
entire support system is usually the family, not govenment. this means that the line between getting things done 
and not getting things done, is usually very thin. this creates the exixtent pressure to make money individually, 
and to make enough of it. i had no real skillset. Its okay, i had to postpone that dream till half a decade later
to get into something else that will fund that infrastructure. Meanwhile most of the people i finished secondary
school with were already writing jambs, getting into schools and taking courses they never planned to take. they 
thought they didnt have an option - at least it's better than rewriting jamb for years, trying to get your desired 
course, or worse, not having a good waec to start with. common as these names sound, entire destinies depend on them 
for education. The lie is inherited, quietly over generations. That there is nothing else we can do. its painful 
because many other place in the world, things seem progressive. but my own place don't seem okay. the difficulty 
and manual repetition of things. it is like everything is stuck. I know nation state system and human right is 
fucked in my country, that one is true, people talk about it always. but when it was time to do something about it, 
they all hide behind and waited on the government to do it for them. the lie is generally that there is nothing 
we can do about things. that someone else has to do our things for us. and even when it is clear that we should 
do it, we still need permission to go ahead with things. i needed money for basic survival because the system 
didn't provide the infrastructures for me, so it is a basic need. now i do not understand that i have to spend 
an unimaginable amount of time in some unspeakable environmental conditioning just to get the same certificates 
already owned by 10s of millions of people who currently suffer everyday because the system to validate it is 
in question. i do not see how it even answers the poverty question. why not use the same amount of time to specialise 
into something that we can actually control? i sure had questions. and as i tried for answers, all i found was more 
questions, and silence. the lie is carried not just in the pauses between words, in the expressions that signal 
disappointment, in the rules of who we are allowed to be. By the time we gain ability to reflect conciously, 
it is already ecnoded in our habits, our hesitations, our very sense of probability. we consult, measure and defer 
before we even act. The consequense is profound. a life lived under constant unseen surveillance, where every 
choice is weighed against imagined judgement. People surrender their own agency and call it prudence. they shrink 
themselves and call it respect. They defer their instincts and call it patience. And the cost is subtle but relentless. 
the slow erosion of self trust, the persistent whisper that "I am not enough until i do this or that." This is the 
permission lie - the idea that life itself is conditional, that freedom must be earned externally before it can 
be claimed internally. But the truth is simpler, though harder to accept. no one can grant you the right to live 
your life. That right exists before approval. it exists by nature. it exists simply because i exist. The problem 
is that i often forget this. i wait. We hesitate. We rehearse and second-guess, as though some external authority 
must nod before the next step can be taken. And in the waiting, we live half-lives, small lives, or borrowed lives. 
We inherit dreams we did not choose, patterns we did not write, scripts we did not approve — and we call them 
reality. This chapter traces the invisible cords of that lie, show how it governs behavior without consent, and 
reveal how the quiet rebellion of self-recognition begins. Because the first step toward freedom is understanding 
that the only permission you ever need is the one you give yourself.

How Society Trains People to Wait

From the earliest years, humans are quietly conditioned to depend on external authority. It begins in ways that 
feel ordinary, even natural: a teacher’s nod validates an answer, a parent’s approval sanctions a decision, a 
religious doctrine defines which desires are acceptable, and as adults, society itself seems to hold the key 
to what success or legitimacy looks like. Every significant move is framed as requiring someone “above,” louder, 
or officially recognized to grant permission. Only then may action be taken.
Over time, these patterns become internalized. The external becomes internal. We carry the ‘expectation of 
approval as an automatic template, a mental habit that whispers before we act. To wait, check, defer, seek sanction. 
This template is invisible, but it shapes the way we think and the choices we allow ourselves to imagine. It is why 
so many of us begin with “I can’t” before we ever ask, “How can I?” Because restriction is assumed. Permission is 
treated as a prerequisite. And yet, no one actually owns the right to grant it. The authority we defer to is 
often a construct, a system of inherited expectations rather than a living arbiter of our lives. The problem is 
not the figures themselves, but the conditioning that convinces us their voice is necessary before our own. In 
practice, this teaches generations to hesitate, to wait, and sometimes to abandon the pursuit entirely—not 
because the world denies them, but because they have learned to deny themselves first.

The Psychology Behind Needing Authorization

When humans confuse validation with direction, and instruction with destiny, a subtle but powerful fear begins 
to take hold. From childhood onward, the mind learns to associate action with oversight. A teacher’s nod, a
parent’s approval, a societal or religious signal—they are not merely external guides; they become the template 
by which we measure whether movement is safe, acceptable, or even possible. Over time, this template hardens 
into habit, and the mind begins to wait—not because the world demands it, but because it has been trained to 
assume that without explicit authorization, action is forbidden. Fear anchors itself at the intersection of 
responsibility and visibility. To initiate is to step into the unknown, to risk judgment, to expose oneself 
to consequences, to risk being wrong. And the ego — the subtle, persistent engine that seeks comfort, coherence, 
and continuity — does not welcome such exposure lightly. Waiting, then, becomes the default strategy. By 
deferring, we avoid the possibility of error. By pausing, we preserve the self-image we have constructed. 
Permission becomes not a requirement but a form of emotional armor. It is the shield we carry into life to 
reduce anxiety, to manage risk, and to protect the fragile sense of competence we believe depends on others’ 
approval. The consequences of this conditioning are profound. When action is tied to external authorization, 
initiative becomes costly—not because the external world punishes it, but because the internal world interprets
it as dangerous. The very act of trying, of moving forward, becomes entangled with fear: fear of failure, 
fear of criticism, fear of disrupting the fragile equilibrium of identity we have inherited and constructed. 
Over time, hesitation is internalized. The mind learns to anticipate risk not objectively, but through the 
lens of permission — or the lack thereof. This creates a predictable cycle. Waiting feels safe. Waiting prevents 
embarrassment, conflict, or mistake. Waiting allows the ego to remain intact. But it also shapes perception 
in more subtle ways. When waiting becomes a habit, the mind stops asking how and starts assuming cannot. 
“I can’t” becomes reflexive, preceding curiosity, effort, or experimentation. The world itself does not 
impose restriction; the learned expectation of restriction does. Action, creativity, and self-determination 
shrink under the weight of invisible authority. And yet, this conditioning is often invisible. It operates 
silently, masked as prudence, respect, or discipline. It masquerades as wisdom, as patience, or even as humility. 
But the truth is sharper: it is fear dressed in culturally approved clothing. Fear of exposure. Fear of 
misjudgment. Fear of living a life fully owned. Waiting is a defense mechanism, not a virtue. Permission 
is an illusion — the ego’s way of softening reality so that it remains undisturbed, so that risk appears 
negotiable, and the self remains seemingly protected from consequence. Recognition changes the dynamic. 
The moment we notice this internalized pattern—this dependence on authorization—we are confronted with 
a simple, unavoidable truth: no one can grant permission to live your life. Authority is a perception, not 
a mandate. The only voice that truly matters is the one that speaks from within, the one that has been 
quieted beneath layers of inherited expectation, cultural conditioning, and the fear of visibility.
This realization is both terrifying and liberating. Terrifying, because it exposes just how long we have 
lived waiting. Liberating, because it reveals the power we have always carried—the ability to move, decide, 
and act without asking, without seeking consent, without deferring. Once this truth is visible, the habit 
of waiting loses its subtle authority. Hesitation becomes a choice, not a default. Permission becomes optional, 
not required. In this light, waiting is no longer neutral. Every delay, every deferment, is a small surrender 
of agency, a quiet surrender to the illusion that someone else’s nod is needed to validate existence. 
And the deeper the pattern has been internalized, the longer it takes to undo.But undo it we can. 
Because awareness strips the illusion of its power. Once we recognize the mechanics of permission—the fear 
it masks, the scripts it enforces, the false authority it projects—we are free to act without it. We are free 
to reclaim our own direction, to initiate, to risk, to be wrong, and to be fully seen.
And this is the first, essential step toward living a life authored by self, not by expectation.

Childhood Conditioning: The Hidden Curriculum

Long before a person begins to question the world, they are already being trained on how to exist within it. 
Not through formal instruction alone, but through a quieter, more pervasive process—a hidden curriculum that 
shapes behavior, identity, and expectation without ever announcing itself as such. In school, you raise your 
hand before speaking. Not because you have nothing to say, but because expression must be authorized. At home, 
you ask before exploring, before deciding, before becoming. Not because curiosity is wrong, but because 
autonomy is regulated. In religious settings, morality is often presented not as something to be discovered, 
but as something already defined—fixed, external, and unquestionable. Across these spaces, a consistent 
message is reinforced: initiative is secondary to obedience. And the reinforcement is not always gentle. 
In many environments—particularly in parts of the world where authority is deeply tied to hierarchy, age, 
and control — disobedience is not merely corrected, it is punished. Sometimes harshly. Sometimes physically. 
The lesson is not subtle. It is direct, embodied, and unforgettable: do not act without approval. Do not 
question the structure. Do not move ahead of permission. Over time, these repeated signals accumulate. 
What begins as instruction becomes conditioning. What is first enforced externally becomes internal expectation. 
The child learns not only what to do, but how to think about doing. They learn to pause before acting, 
to check before deciding, to defer before initiating. And eventually, this process becomes automatic.
By adulthood, the pattern is no longer visible as training—it is experienced as personality. People describe 
themselves as cautious, respectful, disciplined, or patient, without recognizing how much of that identity was 
shaped by early conditioning around authority and permission. Beneath these labels often lies something simpler: 
hesitation that was taught early and repeated often. This is where the deeper consequence begins to show. 
Many adults do not actively choose the direction of their lives — they wait for it to be validated. They 
look outward for signals that it is “time,” that it is “allowed,” that it is “safe.” They delay decisions, 
postpone action, and second-guess instinct, not because they lack ability, but because the internal template 
still expects approval. It is not a lack of intelligence. It is the persistence of conditioning.
There is also a quiet realization that often comes with maturity. As children, many of us assume that adults 
possess a kind of complete understanding—that their strictness, their certainty, their enforcement of rules 
must come from knowledge we have yet to acquire. There is a belief that growing up will reveal the logic 
behind the rigidity. That one day, it will all make sense. But adulthood reveals something more complex. 
The certainty we once perceived is often inherited, not discovered. The strictness we experienced is frequently 
a continuation, not an exception. What we were taught was not always the result of deep understanding, but 
the repetition of what was taught before. A cycle, maintained across generations, rarely questioned because 
it feels normal to those within it. Even the justifications begin to shift under scrutiny. Phrases like 
“it’s for your own good” begin to lose their clarity. Sometimes they are true. But often, they serve another 
function — to maintain order, to preserve authority, to prevent disruption. The line between guidance and 
control becomes less distinct. What was presented as protection can, in some cases, reveal itself as preservation 
of structure. This pattern is not limited to the family or the classroom. It extends into broader systems. 
Institutions, organizations, and even economies adopt similar language — framing compliance as benefit, 
alignment as necessity, participation as personal gain. And while these systems may indeed provide value, 
it becomes important to recognize the underlying dynamic: not everything that is presented as “for you” 
is primarily designed with you in mind. 

The result is a continuity of conditioning. From childhood into adulthood, across different domains of life, 
the same core message is reinforced in different forms: wait, defer, align, seek approval. And because this 
message has been repeated in so many contexts, it begins to feel like reality itself. But it is not reality. 
It is training.And once it is seen as such, it can be questioned. Because the same awareness that reveals 
the pattern also creates distance from it. It allows a person to ask: what was taught that still serves me 
— and what was simply passed down? Where am I acting from clarity, and where am I still responding to an old 
instruction? This is where the hidden curriculum begins to lose its hold. Not through rejection of everything 
that was learned, but through conscious examination. Through the willingness to separate guidance from control, 
structure from limitation, and inherited behavior from chosen action. Because the moment you see that you were 
trained to wait… you are no longer obligated to continue waiting.

The Myth of Readiness

There is a quiet assumption that governs much of human hesitation — the belief that there exists a state of 
complete readiness. A moment where uncertainty disappears, confidence becomes total, and the path ahead feels 
clear enough to justify action. Many people spend years, sometimes entire lifetimes, waiting for this moment 
to arrive. It never does. No human being ever feels fully ready to step into the unknown. Not because they 
are incapable, but because readiness, as it is commonly imagined, is an illusion. Life does not present itself 
in completed forms. It does not wait for internal certainty before demanding movement. It unfolds in conditions 
of partial understanding, incomplete preparation, and unavoidable risk. And yet, both society and the 
illusion engine reinforce a different narrative. They whisper, subtly but persistently: prepare a little more… 
refine yourself a little further… wait until the timing is right. These suggestions appear reasonable, even 
responsible. After all, preparation is valuable. Growth is necessary. Improvement is desirable. But beneath 
this surface lies a distortion. The line between preparation and avoidance begins to blur. What starts as a 
genuine effort to improve gradually becomes a delay mechanism. The mind shifts from asking, “Am I capable of 
beginning?” to insisting, “I must become more before I begin.” And because “more” is undefined, it becomes 
endless. There is always another skill to acquire, another flaw to correct, another condition to perfect.
This is where perfection quietly replaces permission. Instead of waiting for someone else to say “you may proceed,” 
the individual begins to impose an internal standard so high that it serves the same function. Action is postponed, 
not because it is impossible, but because it does not meet an imagined threshold of readiness. The result is the 
same: hesitation, delay, inaction. The illusion engine thrives in this space. It disguises fear as preparation. 
It reframes avoidance as responsibility. It convinces the individual that waiting is progress, that refining is 
movement, that thinking is doing. And because these activities feel productive, the delay becomes difficult to detect.
But time does not pause for preparation. Opportunities do not wait for perfection. Life continues to move, 
with or without participation. And in that movement, something subtle but significant is lost — not just time, 
but momentum, confidence, and the willingness to begin. This is why the myth of readiness is so costly.
Failure, when it happens, is visible. It teaches, it corrects, it forces adaptation. It is uncomfortable, 
but it is active. It moves a person forward, even if indirectly. But waiting — endless, justified waiting — 
produces no such movement. It preserves the illusion of safety while quietly eroding possibility. Nothing is 
risked, but nothing is gained. The life that could have been explored remains untested, not because it was 
impossible, but because it was never attempted. 
In this way, the myth of readiness destroys more potential than failure ever could. Not through dramatic collapse, 
but through quiet postponement. Through the slow accumulation of “not yet.” Through the belief that one day, 
everything will align perfectly — and that only then will action make sense. But reality offers no such guarantee.
The truth is simpler, and far less comfortable: readiness is not a prerequisite for action — it is often the result 
of it. Clarity does not always come before movement; it emerges through movement. Confidence is not built in isolation; 
it is built through engagement, through error, through correction, through repetition. To wait for readiness is to 
misunderstand how growth works. There will always be uncertainty. There will always be risk. There will always be 
reasons to delay. The question is not whether those conditions can be eliminated, but whether action can occur in 
spite of them. And this is where the shift begins. The moment a person stops asking, “Am I ready?” and starts asking, 
“Am I willing?” something changes. The standard moves from perfection to participation. From certainty to decision. 
From waiting to movement. Because in the end, readiness is not something you find. It is something you create, by beginning.

The Power Lost in Waiting

Every time action is delayed without necessity, something subtle is lost. Not externally, not immediately — but 
internally, where it is harder to measure and easier to ignore. Self-trust begins to erode. Each postponed decision 
sends a quiet signal inward: I cannot rely on myself to move. It may not feel significant in isolation, but over time, 
these signals accumulate. The mind starts to hesitate not because it lacks direction, but because it has learned, 
through repetition, that intention does not always lead to action. A gap forms—small at first—between what is known 
and what is done. And within that gap, confidence weakens. Identity follows the same pattern. A person is not only 
shaped by what they think, but by what they consistently do. When action is repeatedly deferred, the sense of self 
becomes unstable. There is a version of who one believes they are — capable, aware, willing — and another version 
reflected in behavior — hesitant, waiting, uncertain. The distance between these two begins to widen, and with it 
comes a quiet fragmentation. Not dramatic, not visible, but persistent. A feeling of being slightly out of alignment
with oneself. This is the hidden cost of waiting. It is not just time that is lost, but coherence. The internal 
unity between thought, intention, and action begins to loosen. And the longer this continues, the more natural it 
feels. Waiting becomes familiar. Hesitation becomes identity. The absence of movement begins to feel like stability. 
On the surface, this can appear comfortable. Life becomes lighter in a certain sense—fewer risks, fewer disruptions, 
fewer immediate consequences. There is safety in postponement, a kind of quiet ease that comes from not stepping forward.
But that ease carries a cost. Because what is gained in comfort is lost in depth. What is preserved in safety is 
diminished in vitality. A life built around waiting may feel controlled, but it also begins to feel empty. Not because 
nothing is happening, but because nothing is being fully engaged. Potential remains abstract. Desire remains untested. 
Direction remains imagined. And slowly, almost unnoticed, something within begins to fade — not ability, not intelligence, 
but the willingness to move without certainty. The willingness to act without guarantee. The willingness to trust oneself 
in motion. This is where the realization begins to settle. The pattern is no longer abstract. It becomes visible in 
moments — the hesitation before speaking, the delay before starting, the quiet negotiation with oneself that ends in 
postponement. And within that awareness, something sharper emerges: The lie was never truly about permission.
No one was holding it in the way it once seemed. No authority was constantly present, withholding access to life. 
The structure existed, the conditioning was real — but beneath it all, something else was driving the behavior.
Fear. Not loud, not obvious, but disguised. Refined. Reframed. Fear presented as patience. Fear presented as preparation.
Fear presented as “not yet.” And once this is seen clearly, it becomes difficult to return to the illusion completely. 
The language may remain, the habits may persist for a time, but the awareness changes everything. Waiting is no longer 
neutral. It is no longer invisible. It is recognized for what it is—a choice, often made unconsciously, to remain where 
one is rather than risk what comes next. And in that recognition, something shifts. Because the moment waiting is seen 
for what it is, it begins to lose its power.
