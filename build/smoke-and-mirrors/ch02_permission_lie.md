THE PERMISSION LIE

One of the deepest, most subtle prisons I have lived inside is the belief that there is nothing I can do about my own situation — that I cannot act, choose, or become anything without permission first. I was taught this early, without words most of the time, just through tone, expectation, and example: that my desires, my instincts, my sense of self had to pass through someone else's approval before they counted. A parent. A teacher. The community. Later, even the imagined judgment of a bank balance.

When I finished secondary school, things were already moving fast around me, and I already knew more than the certificate suggested. I had learned things on my own, from school and from the internet both. My WAEC result carried nine credits. I had already been exposed to blockchain and the basics of computing. And still, there didn't seem to be much I could actually do about any of it, because so much of what I wanted came down to money I didn't have. All I wanted was IT — computing, building, the actual work — not state service, not any of the paths everyone assumed for me. But on the streets around me, computing had already been mystified into something closer to crime, something people only reached for when they wanted to make money quickly and dishonestly. So when I asked my family for a laptop, my father said no. He didn't raise his voice. He has never needed volume to close a door. He just said, "No. Not that," the way other men say grace before a meal — final, practiced, not meant to be argued with. Not out of cruelty. Out of fear that the only reason a young man wants a laptop and quiet and internet access is to chase the yahoo-yahoo trend everyone around us had already watched ruin somebody's son. I don't blame him for that fear, even now. He never got the chance to ride his own era's biggest trend either — computing was already happening somewhere in his time too, but he was too busy surviving so that I could eventually get the chance to live.

Where I'm from, family is the entire support system. Not government. Which means the line between getting something done and not getting it done is often razor-thin, and it creates a constant, low-grade pressure to make money on your own, fast, by any door that opens. I had no real skill set yet. So I postponed the dream — told myself it could wait half a decade, until I built something else strong enough to fund it myself. Most of my classmates were already writing JAMB, getting placed into courses they'd never actually chosen, telling themselves they had no other option — that it was still better than rewriting JAMB for years, or ending up without a good WAEC at all. Entire destinies get built on results that, common as they sound, decide whether a person gets to choose anything else for the rest of their life.

The lie gets inherited quietly, across generations: that there's nothing else we can do. It's a painful thing to carry, because in so many other places in the world, things move forward. My own place doesn't move the same way. Everything stays stuck in difficulty and manual repetition, generation after generation. I know the nation-state system around me is fucked — everyone here knows it, everyone talks about it constantly — but when it's actually time to do something about it, almost everyone hides behind the same excuse and waits on a government that has shown no sign of arriving. The lie, at its root, is that someone else has to fix our lives for us, and that even when it's obvious we should act ourselves, we still need permission first.

I needed money for basic survival, because the system around me never built the infrastructure I needed. That's not ambition. That's a basic need. And still I couldn't see the sense in spending years inside unbearable conditions just to earn the same certificate already held by tens of millions of people currently suffering anyway, because the system meant to validate that certificate is itself in question. I couldn't see how that path even answered the actual question — poverty — better than spending the same years specializing in something I could actually control. I had questions. And every time I went looking for answers, I found more questions, and silence underneath them.

The lie doesn't only live in words. It lives in the pauses between them — in the small expressions of disappointment, in the unwritten rules of who I'm allowed to become. By the time I was old enough to reflect on any of it consciously, it was already encoded into my habits, my hesitations, my sense of what was even probable. I learned to consult, to measure, to defer, before I ever acted. The cost of that is quiet but constant: a life lived under invisible surveillance, every choice weighed against an imagined judgment that may not even be watching. People hand over their own agency and call it prudence. They shrink themselves and call it respect. They defer their own instincts and call it patience. And underneath all of it, the same whisper keeps repeating: I am not enough until I do this, or that, or the next thing.

This is the permission lie — the idea that life itself is conditional, that freedom has to be earned externally before it can be claimed internally. The truth is simpler, even if it's harder to accept: no one can grant me the right to live my own life. That right exists before any approval does. It exists by nature. It exists simply because I exist. The problem is that I forget this constantly. I wait. I rehearse and second-guess, as if some authority outside me has to nod before I'm allowed to take the next step. And in all that waiting, I live a smaller life than the one I actually have — inheriting dreams I never chose, repeating scripts I never approved, and calling the result reality.

What follows in this chapter traces the invisible cords of that lie — how it governs behavior without ever asking for consent, and how the quiet rebellion of seeing it clearly finally begins. Because the first real step toward freedom is understanding one thing: the only permission I will ever actually need is the one I give myself.

How Society Trains People to Wait

From my earliest years, I was quietly conditioned to depend on authority outside myself. It started in ways that felt ordinary, even natural: a teacher's nod validated an answer, a parent's approval sanctioned a decision, a religious doctrine decided which desires were acceptable, and later, society itself seemed to hold the key to what success or legitimacy even looked like. Every significant move got framed as needing someone above me, louder than me, or officially recognized, to grant permission first. Only then could I act.

I remember putting my hand up once with a question that had nothing to do with that day's topic, and being told, not unkindly, to save it for after the syllabus was finished. The syllabus never finished early enough for the question to come back around. I don't think the teacher even remembers saying it. I remember it because it was the first time I noticed the shape of the cage, even though I didn't have a name for it yet — the quiet rule that curiosity has to wait its turn behind whatever the institution decided mattered first.

Over time, this pattern settled inward. The external became internal. I started carrying the expectation of approval as an automatic template — a mental habit that whispered before I moved: wait, check, defer, seek sanction. The template stayed invisible, but it shaped what I let myself even imagine. It's why I so often started with "I can't" before I ever asked "How can I?" Because restriction was assumed. Permission was treated as a prerequisite. And yet no one actually owns the right to grant it. The authority I deferred to was mostly a construct — a system of inherited expectation, not a living judge of my actual life. The problem was never the people themselves. It was the conditioning that convinced me their voice mattered more than mine. In practice, this is how generations learn to hesitate, to wait, sometimes to abandon the pursuit entirely — not because the world denied them, but because they had already learned to deny themselves first.

The Psychology Behind Needing Authorization

When I confuse validation with direction, and instruction with destiny, a subtle but powerful fear takes hold. From childhood, my mind learned to attach action to oversight. A teacher's nod, a parent's approval, a religious or social signal — these weren't just guidance. They became the template I used to measure whether movement was safe, acceptable, even possible at all. Over time, that template hardened into habit, and I learned to wait — not because the world demanded it, but because I'd been trained to assume that without explicit authorization, action simply wasn't allowed.

Fear sets up exactly where responsibility meets visibility. To start something is to step into the unknown, to risk judgment, to expose myself to consequence, to risk being wrong. Why does beginning always feel like asking for trouble before anyone has actually objected? Why does the body brace itself before the mind has even finished forming the sentence? And the ego — the quiet, persistent engine that wants comfort, coherence, continuity — does not welcome that exposure easily. So waiting becomes the default strategy. By deferring, I avoid the possibility of error. By pausing, I preserve the self-image I've built. Permission stops being a requirement and becomes emotional armor — the shield I carry to manage anxiety, manage risk, and protect a sense of competence I've convinced myself depends on someone else's approval.

The cost of this is real. When action gets tied to outside authorization, initiative becomes expensive — not because the world punishes it, but because my own mind interprets it as dangerous. Trying, moving forward, starts to feel tangled up with fear: fear of failure, fear of criticism, fear of disturbing the fragile balance of an identity I both inherited and built. Over time, hesitation becomes internal. My mind learns to measure risk not by what's actually there, but through the lens of permission, or the lack of it. The cycle becomes predictable. Waiting feels safe. It prevents embarrassment, conflict, mistake. It keeps the ego intact. But it also reshapes perception in a quieter way — once waiting becomes habit, my mind stops asking how and starts assuming cannot. I can't becomes reflexive, arriving before curiosity, before effort, before I've even tried. The world itself isn't imposing the restriction. My learned expectation of restriction is.

And this conditioning mostly stays invisible. It runs silently, dressed up as prudence, respect, discipline. It wears the costume of wisdom, patience, even humility. But underneath, it's sharper than that: it is fear, dressed in culturally approved clothing. Fear of exposure. Fear of misjudgment. Fear of a life that's fully, visibly mine. Waiting is a defense mechanism, not a virtue. Permission is an illusion — the ego's way of softening reality so it stays undisturbed, so risk feels negotiable, so the self stays seemingly protected from consequence.

Recognition changes the dynamic. The moment I notice this internalized pattern — this dependence on authorization — I run into a simple, unavoidable truth: no one can grant permission to live my own life. Authority is a perception, not a mandate. The only voice that actually matters is the one that speaks from inside me, the one quieted for years under inherited expectation, cultural conditioning, and the fear of being seen.

This realization is both terrifying and freeing. Terrifying, because it shows me exactly how long I've spent waiting. Freeing, because it shows me the power I've carried the whole time — the ability to move, decide, act, without asking, without seeking consent, without deferring. Once I see this clearly, the habit of waiting loses its quiet authority. Hesitation becomes a choice, not a default. Permission becomes optional, not required. In this light, waiting is no longer neutral. Every delay, every deferment, is a small surrender of my own agency — a quiet agreement to the illusion that someone else's nod has to validate my existence. And the deeper that pattern has settled, the longer it takes to undo. But it can be undone. Because awareness strips the illusion of its power. Once I recognize the mechanics of permission — the fear it hides, the scripts it enforces, the false authority it projects — I am free to act without it. Free to reclaim my own direction, to start things, to risk, to be wrong, and to be fully seen.

And this is the first, necessary step toward a life authored by me, instead of by expectation.

Childhood Conditioning: The Hidden Curriculum

Long before I ever started questioning the world, I was already being trained in how to exist inside it. Not through formal instruction alone, but through something quieter and more pervasive — a hidden curriculum that shaped my behavior, identity, and expectations without ever announcing itself as training. In school, I raised my hand before speaking. Not because I had nothing to say, but because expression had to be authorized first. At home, I asked before exploring, before deciding, before becoming. Not because curiosity was wrong, but because autonomy was regulated. In religious settings, morality arrived already defined — fixed, external, unquestionable, not something I was invited to discover myself. Across every one of these spaces, the same message kept repeating: initiative comes second to obedience. And the reinforcement wasn't always gentle. In environments like mine, where authority runs deep through hierarchy, age, and control, disobedience doesn't just get corrected. It gets punished — sometimes harshly, sometimes physically. I remember one particular afternoon clearly, not because the punishment itself mattered much in the end, but because of what I actually took from it. The lesson had nothing to do with the question I'd asked. What I learned was simpler and stuck longer: asking the wrong question at the wrong time costs more than not asking at all. The lesson lands directly, embodied, unforgettable: do not act without approval. Do not question the structure. Do not move ahead of permission.

Over time, these repeated signals accumulated. What began as instruction became conditioning. What was first enforced from outside became an internal expectation. I learned not only what to do, but how to think about doing — to pause before acting, to check before deciding, to defer before initiating. Eventually it became automatic. By adulthood, the pattern stopped looking like training and started looking like personality. I described myself as cautious, respectful, disciplined, patient, without recognizing how much of that description was just early conditioning wearing a flattering name. Underneath those labels, mostly, sits something simpler: hesitation that was taught early and repeated often. This is where the real consequence shows up. So many adults don't actively choose the direction of their lives — they wait for it to be validated. They look outward for the signal that it's "time," that it's "allowed," that it's "safe." They delay, postpone, second-guess their own instinct, not from a lack of ability, but because the internal template still expects someone else's approval first. It isn't a failure of intelligence. It's the persistence of conditioning.

There's also a quiet realization that tends to arrive with maturity. As a child, I assumed adults possessed some kind of complete understanding — that their strictness, their certainty, their enforcement of rules came from knowledge I hadn't earned yet. I believed growing up would eventually reveal the logic behind the rigidity, that one day it would all make sense. Adulthood revealed something more complicated instead. The certainty I once perceived was usually inherited, not discovered. The strictness I experienced was mostly a continuation, not an exception. What I was taught wasn't always the result of deep understanding — often it was just the repetition of what had already been taught before, a cycle carried across generations, rarely questioned because it feels normal to anyone living inside it. Even the justifications start to shift once I look closer. Phrases like "it's for your own good" lose some of their clarity under scrutiny. Sometimes they're true. But often they serve a different function — to maintain order, preserve authority, prevent disruption. The line between guidance and control gets blurry. What was presented as protection can, in some cases, turn out to be the preservation of structure instead.

This pattern doesn't stay inside family or classroom. It extends into bigger systems. Institutions, organizations, even economies adopt the same language — framing compliance as benefit, alignment as necessity, participation as personal gain. These systems may genuinely provide value, but it's worth recognizing the underlying dynamic anyway: not everything presented as "for you" was actually designed with you in mind.

The result is a continuity of conditioning. From childhood into adulthood, across every domain of life, the same core message keeps repeating in different forms: wait, defer, align, seek approval. And because it's been repeated in so many contexts, it starts to feel like reality itself. It isn't. It's training. And once I see it as training, I can question it. The same awareness that reveals the pattern also creates distance from it — enough distance to ask: what was taught that still actually serves me, and what was simply passed down? Where am I acting from clarity, and where am I still responding to an old instruction? This is where the hidden curriculum starts to lose its hold. Not through rejecting everything I learned, but through examining it consciously — separating guidance from control, structure from limitation, inherited behavior from chosen action. Because the moment I see that I was trained to wait, I am no longer obligated to keep waiting.

The Myth of Readiness

There's a quiet assumption that governs most of my hesitation — the belief that a state of complete readiness exists somewhere ahead of me. A moment where uncertainty disappears, confidence becomes total, and the path ahead feels clear enough to finally justify action. I could spend years, even a lifetime, waiting for that moment. It never arrives. No one ever feels fully ready to step into the unknown — not because they're incapable, but because readiness, as it's commonly imagined, is an illusion. Life doesn't show up in completed forms. It doesn't wait for internal certainty before demanding movement. It unfolds in conditions of partial understanding, incomplete preparation, unavoidable risk. And yet both society and the illusion engine keep reinforcing a different story. They whisper, subtly but constantly: prepare a little more, refine yourself a little further, wait until the timing is right. These suggestions sound reasonable, even responsible — preparation is valuable, growth is necessary, improvement is desirable. But underneath, the line between preparation and avoidance starts to blur. What begins as genuine effort to improve slowly turns into a delay mechanism. My mind shifts from asking "Am I capable of beginning?" to insisting "I must become more before I begin." And because "more" is never defined, it becomes endless. There's always another skill to acquire, another flaw to correct, another condition to perfect.

There was a stretch of weeks when the line outside my toast-and-egg stand ran longer than I could serve before the ingredients ran out, and I told myself I'd scale up once I'd properly figured out the supply chain. I never finished figuring it out in the abstract — there was always one more variable to account for, one more version of "ready" just out of reach. Someone two streets over opened a second stand before I'd finished planning mine. The lesson wasn't subtle: the version of me waiting to become ready had just lost the exact opportunity the unready version across the street had already taken.

This is where perfection quietly replaces permission. Instead of waiting for someone else to say "you may proceed," I start imposing an internal standard so high it serves exactly the same function. Action gets postponed — not because it's impossible, but because it doesn't meet an imagined threshold I invented myself. The result is identical either way: hesitation, delay, inaction. The illusion engine thrives in this space. It disguises fear as preparation. It reframes avoidance as responsibility. It convinces me that waiting is progress, that refining is movement, that thinking is doing. And because these activities feel productive, the delay becomes hard to even detect.

But time doesn't pause for preparation. Opportunities don't wait for perfection. Life keeps moving, with or without my participation. And in that movement, something quiet but significant gets lost — not just time, but momentum, confidence, the willingness to begin at all. This is why the myth of readiness costs so much. Failure, when it happens, is at least visible. It teaches, corrects, forces adaptation. It's uncomfortable, but it's active — it moves me forward even indirectly. Waiting produces no such movement. It preserves the illusion of safety while quietly eroding possibility. Nothing risked, but nothing gained either. The life that could have been explored stays untested, not because it was impossible, but because it was never attempted.

This is how the myth of readiness destroys more potential than failure ever could — not through dramatic collapse, but through quiet postponement, through the slow accumulation of "not yet," through the belief that one day everything will finally align, and only then will action make sense. Reality offers no such guarantee. The truth is simpler, and far less comfortable: readiness isn't a prerequisite for action. It's often the result of it. Clarity doesn't always arrive before movement — it emerges through movement. Confidence isn't built in isolation. It's built through engagement, through error, through correction, through repetition. To wait for readiness is to misunderstand how growth actually works. There will always be uncertainty. There will always be risk. There will always be a reason to delay. The real question isn't whether those conditions can be eliminated. It's whether I can act in spite of them. That's where the shift begins — the moment I stop asking "Am I ready?" and start asking "Am I willing?" Something changes. The standard moves from perfection to participation. From certainty to decision. From waiting to movement. Because readiness, in the end, isn't something I find. It's something I create, by beginning.

The Power Lost in Waiting

Every time I delay action without real necessity, something quiet gets lost. Not externally, not immediately — internally, where it's harder to measure and easier to ignore. Self-trust starts to erode. Each postponed decision sends a quiet signal inward: I cannot rely on myself to move. It might not feel significant in isolation, but over time, the signals accumulate. My mind starts to hesitate, not because it lacks direction, but because it has learned, through repetition, that intention doesn't always lead to action. A gap forms — small at first — between what I know and what I actually do. And inside that gap, confidence weakens.

Identity follows the same pattern. I'm not only shaped by what I think, but by what I consistently do. When I keep deferring action, my sense of self becomes unstable. There's a version of who I believe I am — capable, aware, willing — and another version reflected in my actual behavior — hesitant, waiting, uncertain. The distance between those two versions widens, and a quiet fragmentation comes with it. Not dramatic. Not visible. Just persistent — a feeling of being slightly out of alignment with myself.

This is the hidden cost of waiting. It isn't just time that gets lost, but coherence — the internal unity between thought, intention, and action starts to loosen. And the longer it continues, the more natural it feels. Waiting becomes familiar. Hesitation becomes identity. The absence of movement starts to feel like stability. On the surface, this can look comfortable. Life feels lighter in a certain sense — fewer risks, fewer disruptions, fewer immediate consequences. There's a kind of safety in postponement, a quiet ease that comes from never stepping forward.

But that ease carries a cost. What's gained in comfort is lost in depth. What's preserved in safety gets diminished in vitality. A life built around waiting can feel controlled, but it also starts to feel empty — not because nothing is happening, but because nothing is being fully engaged. Potential stays abstract. Desire stays untested. Direction stays imagined. And slowly, almost unnoticed, something inside starts to fade — not ability, not intelligence, but the willingness to move without certainty. The willingness to act without guarantee. The willingness to trust myself in motion.

This is where the realization settles. The pattern stops being abstract. It becomes visible in moments — the hesitation before speaking, the delay before starting, the quiet negotiation with myself that ends in postponement. And inside that awareness, something sharper emerges: the lie was never really about permission. No one was actually holding it the way it once seemed. No authority was constantly present, withholding access to my own life. The structure was real, the conditioning was real, but underneath it all, something else was driving the behavior the whole time. Fear. Not loud. Not obvious. Disguised. Refined. Reframed. Fear presented as patience. Fear presented as preparation. Fear presented as "not yet." And once I see this clearly, it becomes hard to fully return to the illusion. The language might remain. The habits might persist for a while. But the awareness changes everything. Waiting is no longer neutral. It is no longer invisible. It's recognized for what it actually is — a choice, usually made unconsciously, to stay where I am rather than risk what comes next. And in that recognition, something shifts. How many of my own hesitations have I mistaken for caution? How many silences did I call patience when they were really just fear with better manners? Because the moment waiting is seen for what it is, it begins to lose its power.
